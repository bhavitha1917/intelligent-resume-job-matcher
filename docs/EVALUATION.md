# Evaluation (v2)

All numbers below were produced by `python -m src.models.train_supervised` (run of 2026-10-22, ~25 min on
CPU) and are stored in [`reports/results/v2/evaluation.json`](../reports/results/v2/evaluation.json).

## What is (and is not) being measured

The only labelled data is the Hugging Face dataset `batuhanmtl/job_resume_fit`. Its `ai_match_score` is
generated by the dataset authors' automated pipeline; it is **not** a recruiter decision. Every metric here
measures **agreement with that algorithmic label**, bucketed as weak (< 40), moderate (40–69) or strong
(≥ 70). None of it validates real-world hiring suitability. Tests show the software behaves as specified,
which is a separate question from whether the score is valid.

## Dataset facts that constrain the design

| Fact | Value |
|---|---|
| Rows after removing exact-duplicate resumes | 2,383 (of 2,385) |
| **Distinct job descriptions** | **23** (one per category; 22–120 resumes each) |
| Near-duplicate resume pairs (TF-IDF cosine ≥ 0.95) | 12, all within the same job |
| Mean `ai_match_score` by job | from ≈ 19 (Engineering) to ≈ 80 (HR) |
| Rows with required-skill coverage = 0 | v1 features **92.9 %** → v2 shared matcher **10.0 %** |

Because the mean label differs so much by job, a model can score well on a row-level split just by
recognising the job. The v1 feature `job_description_length` takes exactly 23 values, so it identifies
the job directly.

## Protocols

- **A. Row-level stratified split (the v1 protocol)**: 70 / 15 / 15, seed 42. All 23 jobs appear in both
  train and test. Kept only for comparison with v1.
- **B. Leave-one-job-out (LOJO), 23 folds**: the test job is never seen in training. This protocol
  estimates generalisation to an unseen job description, but it has only 23 groups, so results are noisy.
- In both protocols, TF-IDF and the scalers are fit on the training part only. Hyperparameters are the
  fixed v1 values, so nothing was tuned on test data and no validation split was needed for model
  selection. The sentence-transformer is frozen.
- The learned models were **not** re-selected using these results. Random Forest stays the displayed
  experimental model because it was the v1 choice.

### How to read this

1. **The v1 headline number was inflated by the protocol.** Knowing only *which job* a row belongs to gives
   macro-F1 0.648 on the row split, 90 % of v1's 0.719. On unseen jobs, the v1 Random Forest falls to 0.656.
2. **The v2 features do not predict the dataset label better.** On unseen jobs they score lower than the v1
   features (RF 0.603 vs 0.656). v2 was designed for training/inference consistency, explicit missing
   values and no job-identifying features, not for imitating `ai_match_score`. Why v1 features do better
   here was not established. The v1 set includes `resume_length` and raw match counts, which may track how
   the dataset's generator scored resumes; this is untested.
3. **The rubric is not a classifier of this label.** Thresholding its score at the label's 70 / 40 cut-offs
   gives macro-F1 0.36, because the two scales differ. Its rank agreement with `ai_match_score` is moderate:
   Spearman **0.58 pooled** and **0.46 on average within a job**. Nothing in the rubric was fitted to these
   labels.

## Within-job ranking

Design: each of the 23 jobs is one query. Its candidates are that job's paired resumes (22–120), and
graded relevance is the dataset label (weak 0, moderate 1, strong 2). Learned-model scores come from LOJO
predictions, so the ranked job was never seen in training. The ranking scalar for models is
P(moderate) + 2·P(strong). This scalar is used only for ranking metrics and never displayed. K = 10.
Precision@10 and MRR use "strong" as relevant and skip the 5 jobs that have no strong candidate.

| Method | NDCG@10 (graded) | P@10 (strong) | MRR (first strong) | mean within-job Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Required-skill coverage, **v1** matching | 0.407 | 0.217 | 0.321 | 0.304 |

### How to read this

- The corrected skill matcher ranks much better than the v1 matcher (NDCG 0.41 → 0.61; MRR 0.32 → 0.78).
  This is the measurable effect of fixing the training/inference inconsistency.
- The rubric ranks clearly better than random. On NDCG and P@10 it is roughly on par with semantic
  similarity. **Several methods agree with the dataset label more closely than the rubric does**: TF-IDF
  similarity, Logistic Regression and both Random Forests. This is reported, not hidden.
- This does **not** mean TF-IDF or a learned model is the better *product* score. Its rankings would agree
  more with the dataset generator, which is not a ground truth, and it cannot explain itself with
  requirement-level evidence. The app keeps the transparent rubric as the score for that reason, and
  because no human relevance judgments exist to decide otherwise.
- The old v1 ranking evaluation (true pair + 9 resumes from *other* jobs) mostly measured whether a model
  can tell an HR resume from a chef resume. It has been replaced by the within-job design above.

## Limitations that block strong claims

- The labels are algorithmic, with only 23 jobs and one job per category, so job and occupation are
  confounded.
- The rubric weights are a documented judgment and have not been validated against humans.
- There is no calibration. Model probabilities are not shown as percentages and should not be read as
  calibrated.
- Results come from one dataset in English, mostly US-style resumes, and may not transfer to other regions,
  industries or resume conventions (e.g. Indian resumes with personal-details sections, which are handled
  by redaction but are not represented in the evaluation data).

## Recommended next step

Build a small **human-reviewed relevance set**: for example, 10–20 real job descriptions × 15–30 consented
or synthetic resumes each, rated independently by two or more reviewers on a 0–3 scale with written
criteria. Report inter-rater agreement, then evaluate the rubric (and any weight changes) against it with
grouped cross-validation by job. Until then, the score is decision-support evidence summarisation, not a
validated suitability measure.
